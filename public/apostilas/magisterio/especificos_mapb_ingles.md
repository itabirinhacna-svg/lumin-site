## Chapter 1 - Reading comprehension

### 1.1 Reading is not word-by-word translation

Compreender um texto nao exige traduzir cada palavra. Leitores mobilizam genero, titulo, imagens, palavras-chave, conhecimentos previos e relacoes entre frases.

| Strategy | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Skimming | obter ideia geral | ler titulo, inicio e palavras recorrentes |
| Scanning | localizar informacao | procurar data ou nome |
| Prediction | antecipar sentidos | usar titulo e imagem |
| Inference | concluir pelo contexto | deduzir significado de termo |

### 1.2 Main and secondary ideas

Main idea expresses the central point. Supporting details explain, exemplify or justify it. In proof questions, avoid choosing an isolated detail as the main idea.

### 1.3 Genres and context

Ads, news reports, emails, instructions, comic strips and articles have different purposes. Genre knowledge guides interpretation.

### Questions with comments

**1.** Scanning is useful when the reader wants:

A) every grammar rule. B) a specific piece of information. C) only pronunciation. D) to rewrite a text.  
**Answer:** B.

**2.** An inference:

A) must be supported by context. B) is random opinion. C) ignores text. D) replaces reading.  
**Answer:** A.

### Chapter summary

Efficient reading uses strategies, genre and context. Main ideas must not be confused with supporting details.

## Chapter 3 - Verb tenses

### 3.1 Simple present

Uses: routines, facts and general truths.

`Teachers plan lessons.`  
`She studies every day.`

In third person singular, verbs often receive `-s`. Questions and negatives use `do/does`.

### 3.2 Present continuous

Structure: `am/is/are + verb-ing`. It commonly indicates actions in progress or temporary situations.

`They are reading now.`

### 3.3 Simple past

Regular verbs commonly receive `-ed`; irregular verbs have specific forms.

`She visited the school.`  
`He wrote a report.`

Negatives and questions usually use `did`.

### 3.4 Future forms

`Will` can express predictions and decisions; `be going to` often expresses plans or evidence-based predictions.

### 3.5 Present perfect

Structure: `have/has + past participle`. It connects past and present.

`They have studied English for three years.`

> **Pegadinha:** simple past tends to combine with finished time references: `I visited London in 2020`. Present perfect commonly avoids a finished past time marker.

### Questions with comments

**1.** Correct sentence:

A) She study every day. B) She studies every day. C) She studying every day. D) She do studies.  
**Answer:** B.

**2.** "They have lived here since 2022" uses:

A) simple past. B) present perfect. C) future. D) imperative.  
**Answer:** B.

### Chapter summary

Verb tenses express time and aspect. Form and context must be analyzed together.

## Chapter 4 - Voice and reported speech

### 4.1 Active and passive voice

Active voice highlights agent: `The teacher corrected the tests.`  
Passive voice highlights receiver or action: `The tests were corrected by the teacher.`

Passive structure uses `be + past participle`, adjusted to tense.

### 4.2 Direct and indirect speech

Direct: She said, `"I am tired."`  
Reported: She said **that she was tired**.

Changes may occur in tense, pronouns and time expressions depending on context.

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| today | that day |
| tomorrow | the next day |
| yesterday | the day before |
| here | there |

### Questions with comments

**1.** Passive version of "Students read the book":

A) The book is read by students. B) The book reads students. C) Students are book. D) The book reading.  
**Answer:** A.

### Chapter summary

Voice changes focus. Reported speech conveys another person's words with adjustments when required.

## Chapter 6 - Prepositions, phrasal verbs and idioms

Prepositions express relationships of time, place and other meanings.

| Context | Examples |
|---|---|
| Time | at 8 o'clock, on Monday, in May |
| Place | at school, on the table, in the room |

Phrasal verbs combine verb and particle, sometimes producing meaning not predictable word by word: `give up` = desistir; `look for` = procurar; `turn on` = ligar.

Idioms are conventional expressions: `break the ice` means ease initial tension, not physically break ice.

### Question with comments

A) desligar. B) procurar. C) desistir. D) inventar.

### Chapter summary

## Chapter 7 - Clauses and conditionals

### 7.1 Noun, adjective and adverb clauses

| Clause | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun clause | functions as noun | I know **that she studies**. |
| Relative clause | characterizes noun | The book **that I bought** is useful. |
| Adverb clause | expresses circumstance | I stayed home **because it rained**. |

### 7.2 Conditionals

| Type | Typical structure | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Zero | If + present, present | general truth |
| First | If + present, will + verb | real future possibility |
| Second | If + past, would + verb | hypothetical situation |
| Third | If + past perfect, would have + participle | unreal past |

**Examples:**  
`If water reaches 100 graus C, it boils.`  
`If I study, I will learn.`  
`If I had time, I would travel.`  
`If she had studied, she would have passed.`

### Questions with comments

**1.** "If I study, I will pass" is:

A) zero conditional. B) first conditional. C) third conditional. D) passive voice.  
**Answer:** B.

### Chapter summary

Clauses organize complex sentences. Conditionals express truths, possibilities and hypothetical situations.

## Chapter 8 - Teaching English and BNCC

### 8.1 Four skills and language use

English teaching develops reading, writing, listening and speaking in meaningful practices. Grammar and vocabulary support communication; they should not appear only as disconnected lists.

### 8.2 Approaches and methodologies

| Approach | Focus |
|---|---|
| Grammar-translation | rules and translation |
| Direct method | use of target language |
| Audiolingual | patterns and repetition |
| Communicative approach | meaningful interaction |
| Task-based teaching | language use to perform tasks |

Methods are historical references, not recipes. Teacher decisions depend on objectives, context and students.

### 8.3 Culture and citizenship

English is used in diverse contexts around the world. Teaching should avoid assuming one culture or accent as the only legitimate model. Intercultural work compares perspectives and supports citizenship.

### 8.4 BNCC

In the BNCC, English is situated in the area of Languages. The component emphasizes social practices, interculturality, multiple literacies and English as lingua franca.

### 8.5 Assessment

Assessment should align with objectives. Reading tasks, oral interaction, writing processes, portfolios and self-assessment reveal different learning dimensions.

### Questions with comments

**1.** Communicative teaching:

**Answer:** B.

**2.** English as lingua franca:

A) recognizes diverse uses in international communication. B) permits only one accent. C) excludes culture. D) restricts language to one country.  
**Answer:** A.

### Chapter summary

Teaching English combines skills, language knowledge, interaction, diversity and assessment. BNCC emphasizes social and intercultural practices.

## Chapter 9 - Reading practice: context, cohesion and inference

### 9.1 Reference words

Pronouns and demonstratives maintain cohesion. To understand `it`, `they`, `this` or `those`, the reader identifies the most plausible referent by grammar and meaning.

**Example:** `The students read the article. They discussed it afterwards.`  
`They` refers to students; `it` refers to article.

### 9.2 Logical relationships

Connectors guide comprehension:

| Connector | Relationship |
|---|---|
| however | contrast |
| therefore | consequence or conclusion |
| because | reason |
| although | concession |
| unless | negative condition |
| in addition | addition |

**Example:** `Although the task was difficult, the students completed it.` The sentence contrasts difficulty with successful completion.

### 9.3 Meaning from context

Suppose a text states: `The path was slippery after the heavy rain, so the hikers walked slowly.` Even if a reader does not know `slippery`, rain and slow walking suggest a surface where people may slide.

> **Exam tip:** do not translate options mechanically. Test each meaning against the surrounding sentence.

### Attention box - common exam mistakes
- Searching for a single identical word instead of reading meaning.
- Ignoring pronoun reference.
- Confusing `although` with cause.
- Treating cognates as automatically correct.

### Commented questions

**1.** In `The school bought new computers. They will be installed tomorrow`, `they` refers to:

A) school. B) computers. C) tomorrow. D) installation.  
**Answer:** B.

**2.** `Unless you study, you will not improve` expresses:

A) addition. B) negative condition. C) past habit. D) comparison.  
**Answer:** B.

### Chapter summary

Text comprehension depends on cohesive references, connectors and contextual inference, not isolated translation.

## Chapter 10 - Grammar contrasts that often appear in exams

### 10.1 Countable and uncountable nouns

Countable nouns can be counted individually: `book/books`, `student/students`. Uncountable nouns are not normally counted without a unit: `information`, `water`, `advice`.

| Countable | Uncountable |
|---|---|
| many books | much information |
| a few students | a little water |
| fewer tasks | less time |

### 10.2 Present perfect and simple past

Use simple past for finished events at a defined past time: `She visited London in 2022.` Use present perfect when past action connects to present or time remains open: `She has visited London twice.`

### 10.3 Gerund and infinitive

Some verbs are commonly followed by `-ing`: `enjoy reading`, `avoid wasting`. Others are followed by infinitive: `want to learn`, `decide to study`. Patterns should be studied in context.

### 10.4 Relative clauses

`Who` commonly refers to people; `which`, to things; `that` may refer to people or things in many restrictive clauses.

`The teacher who helped me is here.`  
`The book that I borrowed is useful.`

### 10.5 Modal verbs

| Modal | Typical meaning |
|---|---|
| can | ability or possibility |
| may | possibility or permission |
| must | strong obligation or deduction |
| should | advice |
| might | possibility |

Modal verbs are followed by base form: `She can read`, not `she can reads`.

### Attention box - common exam mistakes
- Using `informations`.
- Combining `did` with past verb: incorrect `did went`.
- Adding `to` after modal: incorrect `must to study`.
- Translating present perfect by a fixed Portuguese tense without context.

### Commented questions

**1.** Correct sentence:

A) She gave me an advice.  
B) She gave me some advice.  
C) She gave me advices.  
D) She gave an informations.

**Answer:** B. `Advice` is uncountable.

**2.** Correct structure:

A) He must studies. B) He must to study. C) He must study. D) He must studied.  
**Answer:** C.

### Chapter summary

Exams frequently test contrasts: countable nouns, tense choice, verb patterns, relatives and modals. Meaning and structure work together.

## Chapter 11 - Lesson planning and assessment

### 11.1 Define a language-use objective

A lesson objective should state what students will do with language. `Learn simple past` is incomplete. A stronger objective: `Read a short biography and identify past events, then write a brief timeline using simple past`.

### 11.2 Sequence a lesson

1. activate prior knowledge;
2. introduce genre and context;
3. set a reading or listening purpose;
4. guide comprehension;
5. focus on useful language features;
6. propose meaningful production;
7. offer feedback;
8. assess learning.

### 11.3 Use authentic and adapted texts

Authentic texts circulate socially: posts, advertisements, songs, news reports and instructions. Adapted texts can be useful when aligned with learner needs. The choice depends on objective, not on a simplistic opposition.

### 11.4 Assess different abilities

Reading comprehension, writing, listening and speaking require suitable evidence. A multiple-choice reading test does not fully measure oral interaction. Formative assessment includes observation, drafts, peer review and self-assessment.

### Attention box - common exam mistakes
- Teaching grammar without communicative purpose.
- Using translation as the only procedure.
- Demanding oral production without preparation.
- Assessing skills with mismatched instruments.

### What usually appears in exams

- Communicative approach.
- English as lingua franca.
- Multiliteracies and cultural diversity.
- Alignment between objective, method and assessment.

### Commented questions

**1.** A well-designed lesson:

A) articulates objective, activity and assessment.  
B) uses grammar lists only.  
C) excludes context.  
D) tests speaking only through translation.

**Answer:** A.

**2.** An authentic text:

A) circulates in social practices.  
B) is always too difficult.  
C) must never be adapted.  
D) excludes genre.

**Answer:** A.

### Chapter summary

English teaching becomes meaningful when objectives describe language use, activities create context and assessment matches learning.

## Chapter 12 - Verb system in greater depth

### 12.1 Questions and negatives

English often uses auxiliary verbs. In simple present, use `do/does`; in simple past, `did`. Main verb returns to base form after auxiliary.

| Affirmative | Question | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| She studies. | Does she study? | She does not study. |
| They visited. | Did they visit? | They did not visit. |

> **Common trap:** `Did she went?` is incorrect. Use `Did she go?`

### 12.2 Continuous forms

Present continuous uses `am/is/are + verb-ing`. Past continuous uses `was/were + verb-ing`. Continuous forms typically foreground ongoing or temporary activity.

`They were studying when the lights went out.`

### 12.3 Present perfect markers

`Since` introduces starting point; `for`, duration; `already`, completed event with present relevance; `yet`, common in questions and negatives; `ever`, experience.

### 12.4 Future meaning

English expresses future through multiple forms:

- `will`: prediction, spontaneous decision;
- `be going to`: plan or evidence-based prediction;
- present continuous: arranged future event.

### Attention box - common exam mistakes
- Keeping past form after `did`.
- Omitting auxiliary in questions.
- Confusing `since` and `for`.
- Assuming future is always formed only by `will`.

### Commented questions

**1.** Correct sentence:

A) Did he studied yesterday?  
B) Did he study yesterday?  
C) Does he studied yesterday?  
D) He did studied yesterday.

**Answer:** B.

**2.** Correct use:

A) I have lived here since 2020.  
B) I have lived here for 2020.  
C) I lived here since three years.  
D) I am live here.

**Answer:** A.

### Chapter summary

Auxiliaries organize questions and negatives. Tense choice depends on form and intended meaning.

## Chapter 13 - Structure of complex sentences

### 13.1 Coordination and subordination

Coordinating conjunctions connect elements of similar status: `and`, `but`, `or`, `so`. Subordinators introduce dependent clauses: `because`, `although`, `if`, `when`.

`She studied, but she was tired.`  
`She studied although she was tired.`

### 13.2 Relative clauses

Restrictive clauses identify a referent: `Students who revise learn more.` Non-restrictive clauses add information and are separated by commas: `My teacher, who lives nearby, walks to school.`

### 13.3 Conditionals and meaning

Conditionals must be interpreted, not classified only by formula. Zero conditional expresses regular relationship. First conditional presents real possibility. Second and third construct hypothetical meanings.

### 13.4 Passive voice in context

Passive voice is useful when action or affected entity deserves focus or when agent is unknown: `The school was built in 1990.` It is not simply mechanical inversion.

### Attention box - common exam mistakes
- Using comma without understanding clause role.
- Confusing `although` and `because`.
- Mixing conditional patterns without intended meaning.
- Transforming passive without adjusting tense.

### Commented questions

**1.** In `The book, which was expensive, is useful`, relative clause:

A) adds non-restrictive information.  
B) identifies one book among many necessarily.  
C) expresses condition.  
D) is passive future.

**Answer:** A.

### Chapter summary

Complex sentences express addition, contrast, cause, condition and characterization. Structure serves meaning.

## Chapter 14 - Intercultural teaching and multiliteracies

### 14.1 English as lingua franca

English circulates among speakers with diverse linguistic repertoires. Teaching should prepare students to communicate and interpret varied uses, rather than imitate a single ideal accent.

### 14.2 Multiliteracies

Texts combine words, images, sound, layout and links. Reading a social media post, infographic or video requires attention to multiple modes. Digital texts also demand critical evaluation of source and purpose.

### 14.3 Cultural diversity

Culture is not a list of holidays from a small set of countries. It includes identities, values, everyday practices and perspectives. Comparison must avoid stereotypes.

### 14.4 Classroom application

A project can compare public service campaigns in English from different contexts. Students analyze genre, images, commands, audience and cultural choices, then produce their own campaign.

### Questions with comments

**1.** Multiliteracies:

A) consider multiple modes and media.  
B) reduce reading to translation.  
C) exclude images.  
D) prohibit digital texts.

**Answer:** A.

### Chapter summary

English teaching develops critical participation in diverse social practices. Interculturality and multiliteracies are central.

## Chapter 15 - Phrasal verbs and idiomatic expressions

### 15.1 Meaning as a unit

A phrasal verb combines a verb and one or more particles. Meaning may be transparent or idiomatic. `Sit down` is relatively transparent; `give up` cannot be understood simply by translating each word.

| Phrasal verb | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| look for | procurar | I am looking for my keys. |
| find out | descobrir | She found out the answer. |
| turn on | ligar | Turn on the computer. |
| turn off | desligar | Turn off the lights. |
| give up | desistir | Do not give up. |
| look after | cuidar de | He looks after his brother. |
| pick up | pegar, buscar | I will pick you up. |
| carry on | continuar | Please carry on. |

### 15.2 Separable phrasal verbs

Some phrasal verbs allow object between verb and particle:

`Turn off the light.`  
`Turn the light off.`  
`Turn it off.`

With pronoun object, separation is required in this pattern. Others, such as `look after`, are not separable: `look after the child`.

### 15.3 Idioms

Idiomatic expressions have conventional meanings:

| Idiom | Meaning |
|---|---|
| break the ice | iniciar interacao de forma mais confortavel |
| piece of cake | algo muito facil |
| under the weather | indisposto |
| hit the books | estudar intensamente |

### Attention box - common exam mistakes
- Translating particles literally.
- Separating inseparable verbs.
- Writing `turn off it` instead of `turn it off`.
- Interpreting idiom word by word.

### Commented questions

**1.** Correct option:

A) Turn off it. B) Turn it off. C) Look the child after. D) Give the project up it.  
**Answer:** B.

**2.** `Find out` usually means:

A) continuar. B) descobrir. C) desligar. D) desistir.  
**Answer:** B.

### Chapter summary

Phrasal verbs and idioms must be interpreted as lexical units. Some particles allow separation; others do not.

## Chapter 16 - Reported speech in context

### 16.1 Direct and reported statements

Direct speech reproduces words: `Ana said, "I am tired."`  
Reported speech conveys content: `Ana said that she was tired.`

When reporting from a later perspective, tense may shift:

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| simple present | simple past |
| present continuous | past continuous |
| simple past | past perfect |
| will | would |
| can | could |

### 16.2 Questions

Reported questions use statement order:

Direct: `He asked, "Where do you live?"`  
Reported: `He asked where I lived.`

There is no auxiliary inversion in the reported clause.

### 16.3 Commands and requests

Commands often use infinitive:

`The teacher said, "Open the book."`  
`The teacher told the students to open the book.`

### 16.4 Context matters

Backshift is not mechanical in every situation. If statement remains generally true or reporting context is immediate, forms may remain. Exams usually test standard pattern, but meaning guides analysis.

### Attention box - common exam mistakes
- Maintaining question order in reported questions.
- Confusing `said` and `told`: `told` commonly requires object.
- Forgetting changes in pronouns or time markers.
- Applying tense shift without reading context.

### Commented questions

**1.** Reported form of `She said, "I will study"`:

A) She said that she would study. B) She said that she will studied. C) She told that study. D) She said would studied.  
**Answer:** A.

**2.** Correct reported question:

A) He asked where did I live. B) He asked where I lived. C) He asked where do I live. D) He asked where lived I.  
**Answer:** B.

### Chapter summary

Reported speech adjusts tense, pronouns, time references and sentence order according to context.

## Chapter 17 - Conditionals in greater depth

### 17.1 Zero conditional

Used for general truths or regular consequences:

`If water reaches 100 graus C at sea level, it boils.`

Both clauses commonly use simple present.

### 17.2 First conditional

Used for realistic future possibility:

`If students revise, they will feel more confident.`

Condition clause generally uses present, not `will`.

### 17.3 Second conditional

Used for hypothetical or less likely present/future situations:

`If I had more time, I would read more.`

### 17.4 Third conditional

Used for unreal past:

`If she had studied, she would have passed.`

The action did not happen as imagined.

### 17.5 Unless

`Unless` often means `if not`:

`Unless you practice, you will not improve.`  
`If you do not practice, you will not improve.`

### Attention box - common exam mistakes
- Using `will` in the `if` clause of standard first conditional.
- Confusing second and third conditional.
- Forgetting past participle in third conditional.
- Treating `unless` as addition.

### Commented questions

**1.** Third conditional:

A) If she studies, she will pass.  
B) If she studied, she would pass.  
C) If she had studied, she would have passed.  
D) If she study, she pass.

**Answer:** C.

**2.** `Unless` usually expresses:

A) negative condition. B) comparison. C) past habit. D) addition.  
**Answer:** A.

### Cumulative review

**1.** `Look after` means: A) cuidar de. B) desligar. C) descobrir. D) desistir.  
**Answer:** A.

**2.** Reported question: A) She asked where I worked. B) She asked where did I work. C) She asked where work I. D) She asked where do I worked.  
**Answer:** A.

**3.** First conditional: A) If it rains, we will stay home. B) If it rained, we would have stayed. C) If rains, stay. D) If it will rain, we stay.  
**Answer:** A.

### Chapter summary

Conditionals express truths, realistic possibilities and hypothetical meanings. Form must be read together with time and intention.

## Chapter 19 - Vocabulary development strategies

### 19.1 Word families

Recognizing related words improves reading:

| Base | Related words |
|---|---|
| educate | education, educational, educator |
| develop | development, developmental |
| communicate | communication, communicative |

### 19.2 Affixes

Prefixes and suffixes provide clues:

- `unhappy`: `un-` suggests negation;
- `rebuild`: `re-` suggests repetition;

### 19.3 Collocations

Words frequently occur together: `make a decision`, `take a test`, `heavy rain`, `strong argument`. Learning combinations avoids literal Portuguese transfer.

### 19.4 False cognates

False cognates deserve contextual review: `actually`, `pretend`, `library`, `parents`, `sensible`, `eventually`.
